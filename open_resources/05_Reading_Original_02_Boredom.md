## In Praise of Boredom: Why an Empty Mind May Be a Creative One

**A.** Few experiences are as universally disliked as boredom. It is the restless discomfort of a long queue, a delayed train or a lecture that seems to have no end. For most of history, boredom has been treated as a problem to be solved, and modern technology has offered an apparently perfect solution: a smartphone that can fill every idle moment. Yet a growing body of research suggests that in eliminating boredom, we may be losing something valuable. Boredom, in this view, is not simply the absence of stimulation but a signal, one that pushes us to look for meaning and novelty. Understanding what that signal means, and how we respond to it, has become a serious subject of psychological research.

**B.** Psychologists distinguish between several forms of the experience. 'Calibrating' boredom is a mild, wandering state in which a person is open to new activity but unsure what to do. 'Searching' boredom is more restless and leads people actively to seek alternatives. The most troubling form, sometimes called 'apathetic' boredom, resembles low mood: the individual feels neither the desire nor the energy to change their situation. It is a mistake, I believe, to treat these states as identical, since only the first two appear to have any benefits.

**C.** The most widely cited evidence for these benefits comes from laboratory experiments. In one well-known design, participants were first asked to complete a deliberately tedious task, such as copying numbers from a telephone directory, before being asked to think of as many uses as possible for a pair of plastic cups. Those who had endured the dull task produced more ideas, and more original ones, than a control group who went straight to the creative exercise. Similar results have been reported in studies using other dull activities, from reading lists of numbers to watching a screensaver for several minutes. The researchers concluded that boredom encourages the mind to wander, and that this daydreaming allows unusual connections to form.

**D.** It would be wrong, however, to conclude that boredom automatically makes people more imaginative. The experiments involve small groups of university students, and the creative tasks are short and artificial. Writing a novel or designing a bridge requires sustained effort, not merely a flash of insight, and there is little evidence that bored workers are more productive over months or years. Indeed, chronic boredom at work is consistently linked to mistakes, absenteeism and staff turnover. The laboratory findings are suggestive, but they have been reported in the media with far more confidence than they deserve. Headlines such as 'Boredom makes you clever' may attract readers, but they ignore the many questions the research leaves unanswered.

**E.** Where the research is more persuasive is in explaining why people find boredom so difficult to tolerate. In a series of studies, the social psychologist Dr Helena Marsh left volunteers alone in an empty room for fifteen minutes with nothing to do but think. A surprising number chose to press a button that gave them a mild electric shock rather than sit quietly. Marsh argues that modern life has reduced our capacity to be alone with our thoughts, and that the constant availability of entertainment has made unstructured time feel more uncomfortable than it did for previous generations, a claim that, while plausible, is difficult to test.

**F.** For parents and teachers, the debate has practical consequences. Many educators now argue that children's schedules have become so crowded with organised activities that young people rarely have the chance to entertain themselves. The early-years specialist Tomas Rivera recommends that families build 'unplanned hours' into the week, during which no screens or structured activities are offered. In his view, the initial complaints of 'I'm bored' typically give way, within twenty minutes or so, to invented games and stories. I find this argument convincing, although it is worth remembering that unstructured time is a luxury that not every family can afford.

**G.** None of this means that we should seek out boredom for its own sake. The aim, rather, is to stop regarding every quiet moment as a problem that requires an immediate solution. Leaving the phone in a pocket while waiting for a bus may feel like a small sacrifice, but it creates the conditions in which the mind can drift, reflect and occasionally surprise us. Whether such habits can survive in an economy built on capturing our attention is another question entirely.
