## The Night Shift of the Brain: How Sleep Shapes Memory

**A.** For most of history, sleep was thought of as a period of inactivity, a time when the brain simply switched off to rest. Modern neuroscience has overturned this view. During sleep the brain is remarkably busy, and one of its most important tasks appears to be the processing of memories formed during the day. Without adequate sleep, it seems, much of what we learn is never properly stored. This idea has important consequences in a society where many people regularly sleep less than the seven to nine hours that most adults are thought to need. Surveys in several industrialised countries suggest that around a third of adults sleep fewer than six hours on a typical working night.

**B.** A night's sleep is not uniform. It consists of repeated cycles, each lasting roughly ninety minutes, in which the brain passes through several stages. In light sleep, the body begins to relax and brain activity slows. This is followed by deep or 'slow-wave' sleep, when large, rhythmic waves of electrical activity sweep across the brain and the sleeper is hardest to wake. Finally comes rapid eye movement, or REM, sleep, in which the eyes move quickly beneath closed eyelids, brain activity resembles that of a waking person, and most vivid dreams occur. Deep sleep dominates the early part of the night, while REM periods grow longer towards morning. This pattern means that someone who wakes several hours earlier than usual loses a disproportionate amount of REM sleep, while someone who goes to bed very late misses much of the deep sleep that normally occurs early in the night.

**C.** Each stage seems to make a different contribution to memory. In a series of studies, the neuroscientist Dr Rafael Ortega showed that deep sleep is particularly important for factual memories, such as lists of words or historical dates. During slow-wave sleep, he argues, recently learned information is 'replayed' and gradually transferred from the hippocampus, a small structure that acts as a temporary store, to the cortex, where long-term memories are kept. Students who were deprived of deep sleep after a learning session remembered up to 40 per cent less the following day. Ortega's team also recorded the brain activity of sleeping rats that had recently explored a maze, and found that the same patterns of neural activity observed during exploration were repeated, in compressed form, during deep sleep, as though the animals were rehearsing the route.

**D.** REM sleep, by contrast, appears to support skills and creativity. The psychologist Dr Mei Tanaka trained volunteers on a complex motor task, similar to learning a piece on the piano, and found that their performance improved overnight without further practice, but only if they had experienced normal amounts of REM sleep. Tanaka also reports that people woken from REM sleep are better at solving word puzzles that require unusual connections, which suggests that this stage helps the brain to link ideas in new ways. She points out that many artists and scientists have described solutions arriving after a night's sleep, although she acknowledges that such personal accounts cannot be treated as scientific evidence.

**E.** Not all researchers accept such a neat division. Dr Helen Brookes, a sleep researcher, argues that the stages work together rather than separately, and that the order in which they occur may matter more than the time spent in any single stage. In her experiments, volunteers whose sleep cycles were interrupted, even though their total sleep time was normal, performed worse on memory tests than those who slept for a shorter time without interruption. Brookes believes this finding may explain why people with conditions that repeatedly disturb sleep, such as sleep apnoea, often report problems with concentration and memory.

**F.** The findings have clear practical implications. Students who stay awake all night before an examination may be undermining the very learning they are trying to achieve, because information studied late at night has no opportunity to be consolidated. Short daytime naps, by contrast, can be surprisingly effective: a nap of around twenty minutes improves alertness, while a longer nap that includes deep sleep may strengthen memory. However, naps late in the afternoon can make it harder to fall asleep at night. Regular sleep schedules matter too: going to bed and waking up at consistent times helps to keep the cycles in their natural order.

**G.** Many questions remain. Scientists still do not fully understand why the brain needs to be offline to process memories, or how dreaming relates to learning. What is now beyond doubt, however, is that sleep is not wasted time. Far from being a pause in mental activity, it may be one of the most productive periods of the day.
