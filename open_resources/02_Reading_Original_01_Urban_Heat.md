## Cool Cities: The Hidden Science of Urban Heat

**A.** On a still summer evening, the centre of a large city can be as much as 7°C warmer than the farmland that surrounds it. This phenomenon, known as the urban heat island, was first documented in the 1810s by the amateur meteorologist Luke Howard, who noticed that his thermometers in central London consistently recorded higher night-time temperatures than those in the nearby countryside. Today the term refers to any built-up area that is significantly warmer than its rural surroundings, and it has become one of the most closely studied effects of urbanisation.

**B.** The causes are now well understood. Dark surfaces such as asphalt and roofing tar absorb up to 95 per cent of incoming sunlight, storing it during the day and releasing it slowly after sunset. Tall buildings trap this heat further by reducing the amount of open sky into which it can escape, a property that researchers measure using the 'sky view factor'. Meanwhile, the replacement of vegetation with concrete removes one of nature's most effective cooling systems: plants release water vapour through their leaves, a process called evapotranspiration, which can lower the temperature of the surrounding air by several degrees. Finally, cities generate their own heat through vehicles, air-conditioning units and industry.

**C.** For many years, the urban heat island was regarded as little more than a curiosity. That perception changed dramatically in 2003, when a heatwave across Europe was linked to tens of thousands of deaths. Subsequent analyses showed that mortality was highest not during the hottest afternoons but after nights on which temperatures failed to drop below 20°C, preventing the human body from recovering. Elderly residents living alone on the upper floors of apartment blocks were found to be particularly vulnerable. Since then, public health authorities have increasingly treated urban heat as a medical issue rather than simply a question of comfort.

**D.** The problem is also unequal. In a study of more than 100 cities, researchers found that neighbourhoods which had historically been denied investment were, on average, between 2°C and 3°C hotter in summer than wealthier districts in the same city. The difference was explained largely by tree cover: affluent areas tended to have parks and tree-lined streets, while poorer areas were dominated by car parks, highways and warehouses. Because residents of these districts are also less likely to own air conditioning, they experience the effects of heat most severely.

**E.** Cities have responded with a range of strategies. One of the simplest is the 'cool roof', a surface painted white or coated with reflective material. Trials of reflective road coatings have reduced surface temperatures by several degrees, although critics noted that the reflected light could increase the heat experienced by pedestrians walking on the street. Green roofs, covered with soil and plants, offer the additional benefits of absorbing rainwater and providing habitats for insects, but they are considerably more expensive to install and maintain.

**F.** Trees, however, remain the most widely recommended solution. A single mature tree can provide a cooling effect equivalent to several domestic air-conditioning units, both through shade and evapotranspiration. Medellín in Colombia has attracted international attention for its 'green corridors', a network of planted streets and waterways that officials say has lowered local temperatures by around 2°C. Yet planting programmes face practical obstacles: young trees need years to mature, require regular watering during droughts, and compete for underground space with pipes and cables.

**G.** Looking ahead, most climate scientists expect the urban heat island to intensify as global temperatures rise and as the proportion of people living in cities, already more than half of the world's population, continues to grow. Some researchers argue that the most important changes will come not from individual technologies but from urban planning itself: the orientation of streets to capture prevailing winds, the spacing of buildings, and the protection of existing green spaces. Whether cities can adapt quickly enough remains uncertain, but it is clear that the design decisions made today will shape the comfort and health of urban residents for generations.
