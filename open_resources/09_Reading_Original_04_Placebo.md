## The Power of Belief: What the Placebo Effect Reveals About the Mind

**A.** In medical research, a placebo is a treatment with no active ingredient: a sugar pill, an injection of salt water, or even a fake operation. For decades, placebos were regarded simply as a scientific tool, a way of checking whether a new drug performed better than nothing at all. Yet researchers repeatedly observed something puzzling. A significant proportion of patients who received the placebo also improved, sometimes dramatically. What was once dismissed as a nuisance in clinical trials has since become a field of study in its own right. Today, scientists from disciplines as varied as neurology, psychology and anthropology investigate how a substance with no pharmacological effect can nevertheless change the way a person feels. Their work raises fundamental questions about the relationship between the mind and the body, a relationship that Western medicine has often treated as two separate systems.

**B.** The improvement is not merely imagined. Brain-imaging studies have shown that when patients in pain receive a placebo they believe to be a painkiller, their brains release endorphins, the body's natural pain-relieving chemicals. In patients with Parkinson's disease, a placebo can trigger the release of dopamine, the very substance the illness depletes. These findings suggest that expectation is not simply a matter of attitude: it can set real biological processes in motion. Similar results have been recorded in studies of depression and anxiety, where patients receiving a placebo showed changes in the activity of brain regions associated with mood. In some trials, the size of these changes approached that produced by the real medication, although the effect was usually shorter-lived.

**C.** Not all conditions, however, respond in the same way. Placebo effects are strongest for symptoms that the brain itself helps to regulate, such as pain, nausea, fatigue and anxiety. There is little evidence that a placebo can shrink a tumour, repair a broken bone or lower cholesterol. Critics argue that some apparent placebo responses are not caused by belief at all, but by the natural tendency of many illnesses to improve over time, or by patients reporting what they think doctors want to hear. Separating these explanations is difficult, because a patient who feels better rarely knows exactly why. For this reason, well-designed studies now often include a third group that receives no treatment at all, which allows researchers to estimate how many patients would have recovered naturally.

**D.** Perhaps the most striking discovery is how much the details of a treatment matter. In one series of experiments, the psychologist Dr Anna Lindqvist found that two placebo pills were more effective than one, and that capsules were more effective than tablets. Coloured pills produced different results from white ones, and an injection worked better than any pill. Price, too, played a part: volunteers who were told that a fake painkiller cost a high price reported greater relief than those told it had been heavily discounted. Taken together, these results suggest that patients interpret every feature of a treatment as a clue to its likely power, and that their bodies respond accordingly.

**E.** For many years, it was assumed that a placebo could only work if the patient was deceived. That assumption has now been challenged. In so-called 'open-label' trials, patients are told honestly that they are receiving an inactive pill. Surprisingly, many still report improvement, particularly in conditions such as irritable bowel syndrome and chronic back pain. Researchers believe that the ritual of taking medicine, together with a positive relationship with the doctor, may be enough to trigger the response, even without false beliefs. If this explanation is correct, it would remove one of the main ethical objections to using placebos in clinical practice, since no deception would be involved.

**F.** Belief can also work in the opposite direction. In the 'nocebo' effect, negative expectations produce harmful symptoms. Patients who are warned about the possible side effects of a drug are more likely to experience them, even when they have been given a placebo. In one trial of a cholesterol medication, a large share of the reported muscle pain also appeared in patients taking the dummy pill. This presents doctors with an ethical dilemma: they must inform patients of risks, yet the information itself may cause harm. Some researchers suggest that the solution lies not in withholding information but in the way it is presented, for example by emphasising the proportion of patients who experience no side effects at all.

**G.** What, then, should medicine do with this knowledge? Few doctors would recommend prescribing sugar pills in place of effective treatments. A more realistic goal is to make use of the conditions that strengthen the placebo response: clear explanations, confident and caring communication, and enough time for patients to feel listened to. If the mind can influence the body as much as the research suggests, then the way a treatment is delivered may matter almost as much as the treatment itself.
