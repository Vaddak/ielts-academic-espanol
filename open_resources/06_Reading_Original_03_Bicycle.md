## Two Wheels That Changed the World: A Short History of the Bicycle

**A.** It is easy to forget that the bicycle, now so ordinary that it barely seems to have been invented at all, was once a radical piece of technology. Its development took most of the nineteenth century and involved a long series of inventors, many of whom never profited from their ideas. The machine that finally emerged transformed transport, altered the shape of cities and, in the view of many historians, played a part in changing the position of women in society. Few inventions have spread so widely: today there are thought to be more than a billion bicycles in the world, far more than there are cars.

**B.** The story usually begins in 1817 with Karl Drais, a German civil servant who built a wooden 'running machine'. It had two wheels in line, a frame and simple steering, but no pedals: the rider sat astride it and pushed against the ground with his feet. Drais was motivated, at least in part, by a practical problem. A series of failed harvests had made horses expensive to feed, and he hoped his invention might offer an alternative means of personal transport. The machine enjoyed a brief period of fashion among wealthy young men, but it was heavy, uncomfortable on rough roads, and was soon banned from the pavements of several cities after collisions with pedestrians.

**C.** Several decades passed before the next major step. In the 1860s, the Parisian blacksmith Pierre Michaux and his associates attached pedals directly to the front wheel, creating what became known as the velocipede. For the first time, a rider could move forward without touching the ground. The iron-framed machines were, however, so uncomfortable that they were popularly nicknamed 'boneshakers', and enthusiasm faded within a few years. Nevertheless, the velocipede proved that a rider could balance on two wheels while pedalling, a fact that had previously been doubted.

**D.** Inventors soon realised that, because the pedals turned the front wheel directly, a larger wheel would carry the rider further with each turn. The result was the high-wheeler, or 'penny-farthing', whose front wheel could reach a diameter of 1.5 metres. Associated in Britain with the engineer James Starley, it was fast and lighter than earlier models thanks to wire-spoked wheels, but it was also dangerous. A rider who hit a stone could be thrown head first over the handlebars, and the machine was largely limited to athletic young men.

**E.** The breakthrough came in 1885, when Starley's nephew, John Kemp Starley, produced the Rover 'safety bicycle'. It had two wheels of similar size and, crucially, a chain that transferred power from the pedals to the rear wheel. Gearing meant that a large front wheel was no longer necessary, and the rider sat low enough to put both feet on the ground. Within a few years, almost every manufacturer had adopted this basic design, which remains recognisable in the bicycles of today. The price of bicycles also began to fall as factories adopted methods of mass production, bringing the machine within reach of clerks, shop assistants and factory workers.

**F.** The final element was comfort. In 1888 John Boyd Dunlop, a Scottish vet living in Belfast, developed an air-filled, or pneumatic, tyre, reportedly to make his young son's tricycle ride more smoothly. Although others had patented similar ideas earlier, Dunlop's version arrived at exactly the right moment, and combined with the safety bicycle it created a machine that was fast, safe and comfortable. A cycling boom followed in the 1890s, with millions of bicycles sold in Europe and North America.

**G.** The social consequences were considerable. For working people, the bicycle offered cheap, independent travel over distances that had previously required a horse or a train ticket. For women, it was associated with a new kind of freedom; the need to ride comfortably contributed to a move away from long, restrictive skirts. The American campaigner Susan B. Anthony famously remarked that the bicycle had done more to emancipate women than anything else in the world. More than a century later, as cities search for ways to reduce traffic and pollution, the bicycle is again being presented as a modern solution. Many cities are now building separate cycle lanes and bike-sharing schemes, suggesting that a nineteenth-century invention may yet play a central role in the transport of the future.
