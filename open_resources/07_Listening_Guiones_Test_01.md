**TUTOR:** Remind me why you chose that topic. I don't think it was on my list of suggestions.

**THE HISTORY OF GLASS**

**Origins**
- Earliest glass objects: small **31** __________, made around 3500 BCE

**Industrial era**
- 1900s: a machine produced **37** __________ automatically

**Today**
- Optical fibres carry **39** __________ across oceans
- Glass can be recycled an **40** __________ number of times without losing quality

<details><summary>📜 Guion</summary>

**LECTURER:** Today I'd like to look at a material so familiar that we rarely think about it: glass.

Let's start with its origins. The earliest objects made of glass that archaeologists have found are small beads, dating from around 3500 BCE, in the region of Mesopotamia. People had used natural glass long before that, however. Volcanic glass, called obsidian, was used for tools. Glass can also form naturally when lightning strikes sand. The intense heat melts the sand into tubes of glass.

For thousands of years, glass was a luxury, because shaping it was slow and difficult. That changed with the invention of glass-blowing, probably in the first century BCE, somewhere along the eastern Mediterranean coast. By blowing air through a tube into molten glass, a worker could produce a vessel in minutes rather than days. As a result, glass became cheap enough for ordinary households to use cups and bottles, not just the wealthy.

In medieval Europe, glass had a different role. Coloured glass was used mainly in churches, in the great windows that told religious stories to people who couldn't read. In Venice, the glass industry became so important that, in 1291, all the glass-makers were ordered to move their furnaces to the island of Murano. The official reason was the risk of fire. The city was largely built of wood, but it also made it easier to protect the industry's secrets.

Now let's jump to the industrial era. In the early 1900s, an American engineer developed a machine that could produce bottles automatically, thousands a day, which transformed the drinks industry. Then, in the 1950s, came the float glass process, which is still used for almost all window glass. Molten glass is poured onto a bed of liquid tin. It spreads out and floats, forming a perfectly flat, smooth surface without any need for grinding or polishing.

Finally, glass today. Perhaps its most surprising modern use is in optical fibres, thin strands of very pure glass that carry data as pulses of light. Cables of these fibres run under the oceans and carry most of the world's internet traffic. And glass has another great advantage: it can be recycled an unlimited number of times without any loss of quality, unlike plastic, which degrades each time it's recycled. Unfortunately, in many countries, less than half of glass is actually recycled.
</details>
